How to read a lead card, and how the picks were made

Pick the lead at team preview, then the turn-one line for what they send out. Percentages are how often to click each line; use any randomizer. The two numbers are your win chance after turn one against how the Showdown ladder clicks, then against a player who reads your mix; red means the lead loses turn one.

Each card is one group of real teams, filed under the tab of its nearest archetype; the list above says which card a team belongs to. The four on the card won the most whole-game sims when each of the group’s commonest sixes also brought its own best four against it (both sides switching, every turn played out, endgames included). Its lines are the turn-one plan for that lead.

The game plan under each lead is how the four was played in whole-game sims against the card’s commonest leads (up to six, 32 games each), weighted by how often each lead appears: the usual turn two, when the back row comes in, which Pokémon Mega Evolves, what loses games, and the opposing Pokémon that beat the plan most. It describes the usual game, not a rule. “Adjust the four” comes from the bring matrix: how many points more (green) or fewer (red) bringing one of yours won when their four included that Pokémon, against that card’s average. Seeing it in their six makes it likely they bring it, not certain. Only differences larger than two standard errors and 4 points are listed.

The opponents are real teams: the 8,992 sides with open team sheets in the cached Showdown M-C Bo3 games, split by Battle sim’s ordered core rules (a team takes the first rule its six matches); teams that match no rule make one “other” card per archetype. Each card is modelled on its own teams: its commonest lead pairs (seen at least 5 times, up to six), how they click on turn one and the back rows they bring, and each Pokémon’s commonest set. The four was picked with whole-game sims: full 4v4 battles in a native port of Showdown’s engine, both sides playing each turn’s best mix (switches included) to the end. 30 fours of yours (the ones a team-search sim and real players brought with this six, and other rain cores) played 8 games against every lead of every card; the leaders were re-run on 32 fresh games each; then each card’s top four candidates met the card’s four commonest sixes, each six bringing its strongest four. To find it, every four a six can bring was played against your four (4 games each) and its two strongest re-played 32 games each; the worse of the two counts. A four can only be picked once its own strongest answers have been searched. The turn-one lines were then run with 12 RNG samples per cell in Showdown’s engine with Champions mechanics, the lead’s own back row behind it, and scored with a win model fit on 7,781 games. Only one Pokémon Mega Evolves per battle; when a four has both Swampert and Metagross, its game plan says which one did. Their Indeedee F’s item is mixed at replay rates: Rocky Helmet 48%, Colbur Berry 32%, Psychic Seed 20%. Their leads outside the card’s commonest are not simulated.

How the hits were worked out

Your hardest hit on each threat, worst gaps first. Pessimistic throughout: each threat is at the bulkiest of its most common spreads for the side you hit, with the items that stop a knockout: Focus Sash (Arcanine-H, Pelipper, Whimsicott), Chople on Kingambit, Colbur on Indeedee F. Sitrus is never counted as extra HP, and Sneasler has already used its Grassy Seed (+1 Defense). The threat’s own weather and terrain are up, and Pelipper’s rain for Pelipper’s own hits; a partner’s rain or terrain is not assumed, so Archaludon’s Electro Shot and Swampert’s Wave Crash are shown without rain and Metagross’s Psychic Fangs without terrain. Spread moves take their 25% cut, and Fake Out is left out. Tap a row for every attacker.

Steel Roller only works while a terrain is up (your Indeedee’s, or theirs) and ends it; its numbers assume it works. One-hit KOs only, so “none” can still be a clean two-hit win; the Matchups grid counts those.

How the one-on-ones were worked out

Who wins the one-on-one with both sides picking their best move each turn: your minimum rolls against their maximum, across their most-used spreads, up to three. Switch between no Trick Room and Trick Room up for the whole fight. Tap a row for the numbers. Under each name: its op.gg doubles usage or ladder rank. Grimmsnarl’s screens and Parting Shot are not counted, only Spirit Break.

One-on-one only, Protect left out. Fake Out is in, on each side’s first turn out; it can’t touch Ghost types, and Psychic Terrain blocks it and every other priority move. “Trick Room up” keeps it up for the whole fight. Spread damage is in, partner chip is not; in doubles, chip is what breaks the * wins. Rolls count accuracy as 100%. Their Pelipper, Charizard Y, Torkoal and Tyranitar bring their weather; Grassy Terrain is up against Rillaboom and a seeded Sneasler, and their Psychic Terrain against Indeedee. A partner’s weather or terrain is never assumed on your side: no Swift Swim for Swampert away from Pelipper, Electro Shot charges a turn, and Steel Roller is counted as if it works. Mega Raichu Y’s Zap Cannon always paralyses: your worst luck counts every turn as fully paralysed, and real rolls use Champions’ 1-in-8 chance. Swampert and Metagross are each shown as their Mega; in a battle only one of them Mega Evolves, and it takes its switch-in hit before it does.

Where these spreads come from

Fastest first, raw Speed: rain doubles Mega Swampert’s (Swift Swim) and Tailwind doubles everyone’s on your side. Your six are shaded; the others are the most common opposing spreads on the Showdown ladder (Smogon’s September 2026 Reg M-C stats), named by nature and the Speed points the spread uses. Equal numbers are a coin flip.
